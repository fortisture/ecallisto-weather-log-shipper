The Station VIŠNJAN RADIO ASTRONOMY

Višnjan is a small hilltop village in inland Istria, and an unlikely place to find one of Europe's more productive small observatories. It began in the late 1970s, when Korado Korlević and a few other teachers from the local school founded an amateur astronomy society. In November 1992 that society opened a public observatory, which went on to hold the IAU/MPC observatory code 120 and to discover well over a thousand minor planets. As coastal lighting grew, the observatory moved to darker skies at Tićan, where it works today.

The thread running through all of it is teaching. The Višnjan School of Astronomy has brought students to the site for decades on a simple principle: students are not shown science, they do science — with real instruments, producing data that goes into real archives.

The CALLISTO receiver

Optical astronomy needs clear, dark nights. Radio astronomy does not — it works through cloud, and in daylight, which is exactly when you want to watch the Sun.

e-Callisto is a worldwide network of low-cost solar radio spectrometers. More than 80 stations spread across every longitude mean some part of the network is always in daylight, so the Sun is never unobserved. When a flare accelerates electrons through the corona they radiate at steadily lower frequencies as they climb — drawing a bright streak down the spectrogram over a few seconds. Those are Type III bursts, the everyday signature of solar activity.

This station records 200 channels from 45 to 404 MHz, sweeping four times a second and writing one file every fifteen minutes. Its receiver sits beneath the dormitory building at Višnjan — which is where the name DORM comes from. Observations are processed by the e-Callisto team at ETH Zurich, who screen, classify and publish the network's data for research use.

Croatia-Višnjan recorded first light on 11 August 2024.

People MENTORS & MAKERS
Biographies drawn from an interview with Marko Radolović published by Parentium.
Mentor · Founder
Marko Radolović
Volunteer, student and mentor on Višnjan's road to the stars. When his own former mentor asked whether he would guide secondary-school students through a new programme bringing the Višnjan Astronomical Society into the e-Callisto network, he agreed — and together with Matej Marković set up and adapted the instrument that measures the Sun's radio emission beneath the dormitory at Višnjan. He is drawn to project work with students through the science camp: mentoring young people, and keeping their curiosity going.
Mentor · Founder
Matej Marković
Built and adapted the station's radio instrument together with Marko Radolović, and presented the e-Callisto programme at Višnjan with him. The pair brought the Astronomical Society Višnjan into the network as a contributing station.
Makers
Students of the Višnjan School of Astronomy
The station's hardware, software and this monitoring site are built and maintained by students of the school — the same people who process the observations.
