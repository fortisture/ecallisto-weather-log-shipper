The Station e-CALLISTO STATION

Croatia-Višnjan is a solar radio spectrometer at Višnjan Observatory in Istria, and a contributing station of the worldwide e-Callisto network.

Optical astronomy needs clear, dark nights. Radio astronomy does not — it works through cloud, and in daylight, which is exactly when you want to watch the Sun. The instrument records 200 channels from 45 to 404 MHz, sweeping the band four times a second and writing one file every fifteen minutes, continuously. Its receiver sits beneath the dormitory building at Višnjan, which is where the callsign DORM comes from.

When a solar flare accelerates electrons out through the corona, they radiate at steadily lower frequencies as they climb — drawing a bright streak down the spectrogram over a few seconds. Those are Type III bursts, the everyday signature of solar activity, and catching them is what the station is for.

A single station cannot do that alone: the Sun sets. e-Callisto's answer is more than eighty stations spread across every longitude, so some part of the network is always in daylight and the Sun is never unobserved. Višnjan's data goes to the central archive alongside every other station's, where the e-Callisto team at ETH Zurich screens, classifies and publishes it for research use.

People MENTORS & MAKERS
Biographies drawn from an interview with Marko Radolović published by Parentium.
Mentor · Founder
Marko Radolović
univ. bacc. ing. el. et techn. inf. Electrical Engineering & Information Technology
Volunteer, student and mentor on Višnjan's road to the stars. When his former mentor asked whether he would guide secondary-school students through a new programme bringing the Astronomical Society Višnjan into the e-Callisto network, he agreed — and an electrical and information engineering background proved close to the bone, since a CALLISTO station lives or dies on its RF front end. He is drawn to project work through the science camp: mentoring young people, and keeping their curiosity going.
Mentor · Founder
Matej Marković
Aeronautical Engineering student
Co-builder of the station. Working alongside Marko Radolović he set up and adapted the receiver beneath the dormitory that now measures the Sun's radio emission, and the pair presented the e-Callisto programme at Višnjan together. Between them they took the station from an idea to a contributing member of the network — first files in April 2024, first light that August — and the hardware has been refined every season since, from the amplifier to the antenna feedpoint.
Makers
Students of the Višnjan School of Astronomy
Škola astronomije Višnjan Observers, builders and maintainers
The station is built and run by students. The receiver was assembled and adapted by hand, the antenna's connector shield was designed and printed on site, and the monitoring you are reading now was written to answer a practical question: is the instrument still recording? Students are not shown science here — they do it, with real instruments, on real problems that have no answer at the back of the book, producing data that goes into a real archive.
